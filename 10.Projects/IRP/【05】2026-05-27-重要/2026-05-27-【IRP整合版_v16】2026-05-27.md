# 1. Introduction and Justification of the Topic
`[UPDATED — 增加 Medicine 3.0 内感受萎缩循环框架]`

I am interested in the future of preventive health behaviour — specifically, the future of **dietary feedback design** for individuals striving to improve their nutrition. This is an urgent societal challenge because, unlike physical exercise or sleep, the benefits of good nutrition are almost entirely invisible on a human timescale. A week of rigorous dietary adherence generates no immediate physiological signal, no intuitive score, and no perceivable sense of progress. This phenomenon is not fundamentally a motivational deficit; rather, it represents a profound structural feedback problem. I term this the *phenomenological silence* of eating well.

This silence creates a critical intention-action gap. Human decision-making is heavily influenced by present bias (Kahneman, 2011), meaning individuals inevitably struggle to sustain behaviours that lack immediate, tangible rewards. The dominant digital interventions — MyFitnessPal, Noom, and analogous manual-logging tools — demand significant cognitive effort while delivering only delayed, emotionally detached numerical summaries. The consequence is catastrophic: health and fitness apps retain only 3–4% of users by Day 30 (Business of Apps, 2024). This failure is not merely inconvenient; it is causing demonstrable clinical harm. Punitive tracking paradigms have been shown to exacerbate eating disorder risk: 73% of MyFitnessPal users with an eating disorder report that the app contributed to their condition (Trottier et al., 2017), and NHS hospital admissions for eating disorders rose 8.5-fold between 2013/14 and 2020/21 (NHS England, 2023).

**[NEW — Medicine 3.0 内感受萎缩框架]** A more precise and urgent framing has emerged through deeper research. The proliferation of Medicine 3.0 tools — continuous glucose monitors, calorie-tracking apps, biometric wearables — does not merely fail to help people eat better: it may actively degrade the capacity it claims to support. The mechanism is a self-reinforcing cycle: external tracking substitutes for internal body signals → interoceptive capacity atrophies through disuse → users become more dependent on external data to know whether they are hungry or satisfied → interoception atrophies further. The most health-conscious people become those least able to trust their own bodies without technological mediation. This is not a failure of individual discipline; it is a designed dependency. Wallace et al. (2026) identify *reification of data* — the progressive replacement of direct bodily experience with quantified metrics — as the primary mechanism. The design question therefore sharpens: **what design conditions help people retain or rebuild autonomous body perception capacity as Medicine 3.0 tools proliferate, turning external reference into a calibration tool rather than a replacement tool?**

A design futures approach is essential here, and the timing is critical. The June 2024 FDA over-the-counter clearance of the Abbott Lingo continuous glucose monitor represents not an arrival but an inflection point: real-time metabolic tracking is crossing from clinical tool to consumer product, while the human-centred design language to accompany it does not yet exist. If non-punitive interaction principles are not established in this window, engineering-led paradigms will calcify into the permanent vocabulary of the field (Gaver, 2002). There is a three-to-five-year opportunity to define these principles before they become irreversible.

This project investigates that design gap. It asks how eating well might be made as legible and emotionally resonant as completing a run — and adds a critical ethical constraint: any feedback system must preserve food joy, prevent disordered eating, and actively support *Food Freedom*, the right to make informed choices about eating without guilt or compulsion (Tribole and Resch, 2020). By combining speculative and critical design tools (Dunne and Raby, 2013) with empirical primary research, this project prototypes radically different futures for dietary feedback and argues that the time to define them is now.

# 2. Background: Literature, Practice, Signals and Trends
`[UPDATED — 研究问题精修至3条，新增文献9篇]`

## 2a. Research Questions
`[UPDATED — 从4条宽泛问题精修为3条可测试问题]`

The original four research questions have been refined into three operationalisable *North Star* questions, each directly connected to a primary research method:

**① Feedback Type Acceptance Threshold**
> For the same person, what is the difference — along a spectrum from calorie counts to warm ambient light — between feeling *cared for* and feeling *surveilled*? Where is that boundary, across sensory modalities (vibration, light, music, scent, taste)?

*Core variable:* Emotional polarity of the Prompt (care ↔ surveillance).
*Design significance:* This is the fundamental question for Concept B (The After Glow) — whether embodied, non-numeric sensation can substitute for digital surveillance as a post-meal signal.

**② Body Signal Vocabulary (Emotional Granularity)**
> What words do people actually use to describe satiety, hunger, and post-meal body state? How large and fine-grained is their vocabulary? Does the granularity of language correspond to the granularity of interoceptive perception?

*Core variable:* Emotional granularity (Barrett, 2017; Lieberman et al., 2007).
*Design significance:* If people lack the vocabulary to describe internal sensation, a design prompt asking them to "notice your body" has nothing to land on. The vocabulary is both the *data* and a component of *Ability* in Fogg's B=MAP model (2009). This vocabulary library does not currently exist in the design literature — existing interoception research is clinical, not design-oriented.

**③ Environmental Intervention Resistance Types**
> When participants resist changes to their eating environment (Concept A — spatial arrangement, food placement), what *type* of resistance is it? Three types require three different design responses:

| Resistance Type | Participant Language | B=MAP Variable | Design Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Disbelief** | "I don't think this would work" | Motivation (belief deficit) | Provide visible evidence; lower commitment threshold |
| **Control aversion** | "It feels like being managed" | Motivation (autonomy violation) | Add choice; make intervention invisible |
| **Friction** | "Too effortful, I can't sustain it" | Ability (too many steps) | Reduce steps; embed into existing habits |

## 2c. Research Summary
`[UPDATED — 新增 Moody 2025 因果链空白、Barrett 情绪粒度理论、QS依赖文献]`

*(v15 原有内容全部保留，以下段落为新增)*

**[NEW — 最关键的研究空白：因果方向从未被证实]** A pivotal update to the literature landscape: Moody et al. (2025), in a synthesis of 27 studies, explicitly state that the *causal direction* of the relationship between tracking behaviour and interoceptive decline has never been established. Does tracking behaviour cause interoceptive atrophy, or do people with initially weaker interoception rely more heavily on tracking? This question has not been formally answered. Herman and Mack (1975) and Polivy and Herman's restrained eating theory establish that repeated suppression of internal hunger/satiety signals leads to external cues (visual, numerical, social) dominating behaviour — a dynamic that is structurally identical to the logic of calorie tracking apps, but this connection has never been explicitly established in design literature. This gap is the precise space this research occupies: not filling a known answer, but asking an unasked question.

**[NEW — 情绪粒度与词汇能力]** Barrett's (2017) theory of Emotional Granularity provides a new theoretical anchor for Research Question ②: the precision of a person's emotional vocabulary directly determines the precision of their emotional and interoceptive perception. Low granularity ("I feel bad") corresponds to coarse-grained perception; high granularity ("I feel a heavy, warm settling in my abdomen") corresponds to fine-grained interoceptive access. Lieberman et al. (2007) confirm that affect labelling — putting words to physical sensations — not only describes experience but modulates it: naming a sensation reduces amygdala activation and increases prefrontal regulation. This suggests that developing dietary body-sensation vocabulary is not merely useful *data* for design, but is itself a *mechanism* for improving interoceptive literacy. The vocabulary library project (Research Question ②) is therefore both a research instrument and a potential design intervention.

**[NEW — 量化自我依赖的直接证据]** PMC11445773 (2024), a study of 535 users, provides the first cognitive-behavioural model of quantified-self (QS) dependence as a negative health outcome. PMC6607598 (2019) offers direct behavioural evidence: when 237 users were prevented from wearing their tracking devices, negative affect significantly exceeded positive — demonstrating that tracking dependency is a measurable psychological state, not merely a preference. Jain et al. (2025) present the first meta-analysis of quantified-self behaviour and consumer health outcomes. Together, these three studies establish that tracking dependence is not a design inconvenience but a clinical risk factor — and that its prevention requires deliberate design intervention, not merely user education.

**[NEW — HCI 领域的时代位置]** BCS HCI 2025's "Opportunities and Challenges of Designing for Mindful Eating: HCI and Somaesthetics" is the first formal HCI conference discussion of mindful eating design. ACM DIS 2025's MindEat! explores screen-based eating interventions. Both confirm that this cross-disciplinary design direction has only just begun — this research is entering a conversation at its inception, not its saturation.

**[NEW — 内感受机制文献]** PMC8140607 (2021) establishes *body mistrust* — not merely low interoceptive awareness, but active distrust of body signals — as the bridging mechanism between interoceptive deficit and eating disorder symptoms. The target population of this research is characterised precisely by this body mistrust: they require external validation not because they lack knowledge, but because they have lost trust in their own signals. PubMed 35033582 (2022) further specifies that *hunger/satiety-specific* interoception (rather than general interoceptive awareness) is the most predictive variable for eating disorder risk. PMC8837977 (2022) provides the critical counterevidence: food awareness interventions can measurably improve interoceptive sensitivity. The interoceptive capacity this research aims to protect is therefore both trainable and testable.

## 2d. Conclusion
`[UPDATED — 更新研究空白描述]`

Three critical insights emerge. First, the dietary feedback gap is an architectural design failure — not a user motivation deficit — arising from the phenomenological silence of digestion and, more precisely, from a designed dependency cycle that substitutes external data for internal capacity. Second, existing punitive tracking paradigms cause measurable psychological harm by reifying data and, potentially, by progressively atrophying the interoceptive capacity they claim to support — though the causal direction of this relationship is the central unproven hypothesis of this field (Moody et al., 2025). Third, cross-cultural and clinical evidence demonstrates that awareness-first, embodied feedback can sustain dietary behaviour change more safely and effectively than metric-based surveillance.

Three primary trends are shaping the next decade: (1) the Medicine 2.0 → 3.0 transition is accelerating consumer adoption of non-invasive metabolic sensors, creating an urgent design window; (2) the Intuitive Eating movement is mainstreaming interoceptive literacy as a protective framework against diet culture; (3) HCI and somaesthetics are converging to create a new sub-field of mindful eating design (BCS HCI, 2025), at whose founding moment this research arrives.

The critical gap, now sharper: the causal link between tracking and interoceptive decline is unproven and unasked in design research; no design tool targets interoceptive capacity cultivation as its primary goal; non-numeric sensory intervention (the sensory spectrum from vibration to scent) has never been systematically tested as an interoceptive training instrument.

## 5a. Participants and Data
*(原文 v15 核心不变，以下为修订细节)*

Primary qualitative data will be sourced from **3–5 adults aged 22–45** with prior dietary tracking experience. *(Reduced from 6–8 to 3–5 to allow depth over breadth, given the sensory testing protocols added to Phase 4.)* Current clinical eating disorder diagnosis remains an exclusion criterion. At least one participant from a non-Western cultural background is sought. An additional **4–5 peers** (separate cohort, no prior knowledge of the project) will participate in the Phase 3 mindful lunch group.

## 5b. Research Phases — v3.0
`[MAJOR UPDATE]`

The methodology advances through four sequential phases in which each phase produces data that feeds directly into the next. The arc is: **Observe → Capture → Gather → Test**, ensuring that by Phase 4, each participant has experienced the problem in their own body and can evaluate design responses from a position of lived authority.

| Phase | Key Method(s) | Duration | Primary Research Question | Output |
|-------|--------------|----------|--------------------------|--------|
| **1** | Space snapshot + one meal + semi-structured conversation | 90 min | ② ③ | Environmental Ability map; body sensation vocabulary (guided); failure moment resistance-type data |
| **2** | 1-day photo diary | 1 day | ② | Post-meal body sensation vocabulary (unguided, natural context) — highest-quality lexical data |
| **3** | Mindful lunch group (4–5 peers, separate cohort) | 45–60 min | ② ① | Group vocabulary emergence; first reactions to non-numeric feedback concept |
| **4** | Design probe interview + sensory feedback test | 50–60 min | ① ③ | Sensory spectrum data (care ↔ surveillance); resistance type diagnosis; ideal feedback form |

**Parallel (all phases):** Autoethnographic diary — researcher's own vocabulary and body-map data as comparative baseline.

### Phase 1: Space Snapshot + One Meal + Conversation (90 min)

**Part A — Space Snapshot (15 min):** Environmental audit — mapping the participant's Ability terrain. Which foods are "default reachable" (high Ability), which are hidden (low Ability)? This is the physical baseline for Research Question ③.

*For cook-at-home participants:* photograph and sketch fridge interior, snack locations, counter arrangement. Identify the three "default grab" points in the space.
*For takeaway-primary participants:* visit the habitual eating location; photograph the environment and note phone placement during meals.

**Part B — One Meal + Conversation (75 min):**

*Failure moment (15 min) — RQ③:* "Think of a time you seriously tried to change your eating but couldn't sustain it. What specifically made it hard — too much effort? Felt pointless? Something else?" *(Listen for resistance type: disbelief / control aversion / friction.)*

*Body signals (15 min) — RQ②:* "When a meal feels 'right', how do you know? What does your body feel like ten minutes after?" *(Record every word used to describe body sensation verbatim. Never suggest vocabulary. If they say only "fine", ask: "What does your body feel like specifically?")*

*Tracking tools (10 min) — RQ① prelude:* "If something could tell you about your meal — without numbers — what sensation or feeling would you want from it?"

### Phase 2: 1-Day Photo Diary

Each participant, on the day following Phase 1:
- Photographs the post-meal scene (empty plate, table, hands, room — NOT the food) once per main meal
- Writes 3 lines in a notes app: (1) Where/doing what; (2) Body sensation right now — *own words, no scoring*; (3) Any change from immediately after eating

*Reference vocabulary provided:* 沉/轻/胀/暖/平静/焦虑/满足/空/安定/涌动 — purpose is to model granularity, not direct vocabulary. The gap between provided vocabulary and actually-used vocabulary is itself data.

*Data value:* Highest-quality lexical data for RQ② because there is no researcher present, no social pressure to perform interoceptive literacy.

### Phase 3: Mindful Lunch Group (NEW)

*Format:* One-off session, 4–5 participants (separate cohort from Phase 1/2), 45–60 min.
*Purpose:* Observe how vocabulary emerges (or is suppressed) in a social eating context; collect first reactions to non-numeric feedback concept.

| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0:00–0:05 | Arrival, brief introduction (minimal framing) |
| 0:05–0:10 | Pre-meal written questionnaire: hunger level (0–10); one word for current feeling |
| 0:10–0:30 | Shared meal (no intervention; observe: pace, phone use, food choice patterns) |
| 0:30–0:50 | Post-meal group discussion (within 20-min satiety window) |
| 0:50–1:00 | Optional: show Concept B sketch; record first reactions |

*Post-meal discussion questions:*
- "What words describe your body right now?"
- "Was there a moment when you felt 'enough'? Or did you eat until the plate was empty?"
- "If a prompt arrived 20 min after eating — no scoring, no judgment — just asking you to notice your body: useful or annoying?"

### Phase 4: Design Probe Interview + Sensory Feedback Test (NEW CORE)

*Duration:* 50–60 min (Phase 4 added ~15 min compared to v15 for the sensory test)
*Research Questions served:* ① ③

**Part 1 — Open with their own diary (10 min):** Print 5–7 of the participant's diary photos. "Looking at these — not what you ate, but what happened after — what was your body telling you?"

**Part 2 — Concept A environmental probe (10 min):** Show sketches of plate zoning, food placement sequence, phone-away-during-meals. Diagnose resistance type using the three-question protocol: belief test / control aversion test / friction test.

**Part 3 — Sensory Feedback Test (20 min) [NEW]:**

*Step 1 — Baseline contrast:*
> "Imagine 20 minutes after a meal, your phone shows: 'You ate 1,200 calories — 300 short of target.' Your reaction?"
> "Now: 20 minutes after a meal, a small light on your table slowly shifts to warm orange. No text, no number. Your reaction?"

*Record the first word of each response.*

*Step 2 — Sensory type testing (each type, same questions):*

| Sensory Type | Presentation |
|---|---|
| Visual | Phone mockup: slow warm amber light gradient |
| Haptic | Two gentle phone vibrations, 1-second interval (live demonstration) |
| Auditory | 10-second audio clip (prepare 2–3 options: piano / nature sounds / white noise) |
| Olfactory | Small scent vial (lavender / citrus): "A scent after eating, as a ritual of closure" |
| Gustatory | One small piece of candy (mint / sweet / sour): "A taste that marks the end of a meal" |

*For each type, ask:*
1. "First word — what is this?"
2. "Does this feel like care, surveillance, or something else?" *(1=complete care, 5=complete surveillance)*
3. "Could you accept this in your daily life?"

*Step 3 — Ranking:* "Of what you just experienced — which felt most supportive? Which felt most intrusive or controlling?"
> "Was there one that felt less like a tool and more like a ritual? Or companionship?"
> "If you could combine these elements into an ideal prompt, what would it look like?"

**Part 4 — Open questions (10 min):**
- "If you could design something to improve your relationship with food, what would it do?"
- "What would you absolutely not want it to do?"

## 5c. Data Analysis
*(原文 v15 核心不变，新增分析线C)*

Reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006) applied to all interview and diary data. Three analysis lines:

- **Line A — Sensory Feedback Spectrum (RQ①):** Position each sensory type on the care ↔ surveillance axis. Identify the "tipping point" features — when does a prompt shift from care to surveillance? (Number appears? Frequency too high? Evaluative language?)
- **Line B — Body Sensation Vocabulary Library (RQ②):** Aggregate all vocabulary, stratify by granularity: coarse ("full/not full"), mid ("stuffed/enough/heavy"), fine ("warm settling/lightness behind sternum/hollow calm"). Test correlation between vocabulary granularity and reported interoceptive confidence.
- **Line C — Resistance Type Map (RQ③):** Tally three resistance types across participants; identify representative quotes; map each type to specific Concept A design elements.

# Appendix H — Preliminary Research Data: Outdoor Fieldwork Session
`[NEW — 2026-05-22 户外调研]`

## Overview

A preliminary fieldwork session was conducted on **22 May 2026** as a Phase 3 pilot (mindful lunch group variant in outdoor/shared-picnic format). This session preceded the formal ethics clearance for primary data collection and is treated as **observational pilot data only** — no formal consent forms were used, and participants are treated as informal collaborators rather than research subjects. Data informs protocol refinement rather than formal thematic analysis.

**Core question explored:** How do the "should" and "want" tensions operate in real-time food choice behaviour when healthy and less-healthy foods are co-present?

## Session Design

- **Format:** Outdoor shared picnic, 4–5 participants, approximately 45 minutes
- **Food composition:** Each participant brought their own food; researcher contributed two items deliberately selected to represent the should/want spectrum: one "clearly should" (cherry tomatoes / cucumber), one "clearly want" (crisps / chocolate). All food was mixed together on a shared picnic blanket — no categorisation indicated to participants.
- **Spatial design:** "Want" foods (crisps/chocolate) were placed slightly out of easy reach — requiring deliberate movement to access. Vegetable/fruit options were placed within easy reach. The researcher observed the reaching, pausing, and retrieval gestures without comment.

## Observation Protocol

Eight stages (see `[[关键成果/户外调研最终方案_2026-05-22.md]]` for full protocol):

| Stage | Timing | Focus |
|-------|--------|-------|
| 0 | Arrival | Food arrangement; spatial design |
| 1 | Before eating | Hunger baseline: each person's words verbatim |
| 2 | During eating | Core observation: reach → pause → take / replace gestures |
| 3 | +0–5 min post | First body description — own words only |
| 4 | +5–10 min | Drawing: body sensation mapped as colour/shape (no words) |
| 5 | +10–15 min | Individual enquiry about drawings: "What is this colour?" |
| 6 | +15–20 min | "Did you want to take something and not take it?" |
| 7 | +20 min | Second body description — compare to first |
| 8 | Departure | Casual conversation — most unguarded data |

## Key Methodological Insights (Protocol Refinement)

1. **The hesitation gesture is observable.** The reach-pause-take/replace sequence is visible even in a social setting. It is not necessary for participants to verbalise the "should/want" conflict — it manifests physically. *Implication for Phase 1 spatial audit:* the "default grab" point mapping has direct observational validity.

2. **Drawing immediately post-meal generates qualitatively different data from verbal description.** Participants who said "pretty full" in verbal description used colour and shape combinations suggesting more complex states (dense overlapping marks, pressure gestures). The gap between verbal and visual data is itself informative. *Implication for Research Question ②:* low verbal granularity may not mean low perceptual granularity — it may mean the vocabulary is absent, not the sensation. This strengthens the case for the vocabulary library project.

3. **"Wanted to take but didn't" is the most generative question.** Even in a permissive, non-judgmental shared setting, all participants had at least one moment of self-edited food choice. The stated reasons varied across the three resistance types. *Implication for Research Question ③:* the three resistance types appear in natural eating contexts, not only in retrospective interview recall.

4. **Return journey produces the most candid data.** Post-session, walking or waiting together, participants volunteered information they had not given during the structured observation. *Protocol update:* always allow 10–15 min of unstructured time after the formal session concludes.

## Autoethnographic Note (Researcher Data)

The researcher participated as an observer-participant. The act of holding back from reaching for a food item twice during the session — noting the internal dialogue ("I should have the tomato") — provided the first direct somatic data point: the "should" voice is experienced as pressure in the upper chest; the "want" is experienced as a pull in the hands and jaw. These qualitatively distinct locations suggest that should/want tension may be mappable to specific body regions, potentially informing the body-mapping component of Phase 4.
