
## 1. Project Overview

FoodWitness: The Transparent Supermarket is a design‑research project that treats supermarkets as **public health interfaces** and **food governance infrastructures** in the age of chronic disease, rather than neutral retail spaces or simple “healthy aisles.”[1][2]
The project builds on existing policies, labelling systems, transparency experiments and participatory food governance initiatives, and asks how, by 2050, supermarkets could turn food evidence into public transparency, healthier everyday choices and shared accountability.[3][4]

### 1.1 Core Idea

The core proposition is that food should not only be labelled but also explained; price should not only be charged but also justified; and consumers should not only shop but also act as **food citizens** who participate in governance through public evidence rather than purchasing power alone.[5][6][7]

The project’s design work is concentrated in a speculative but evidence‑grounded scenario: **The Transparent Supermarket**, organised into seven spatial zones that operationalise food evidence, explanation, sorting, governance and trust.

***

## 2. Problem Framing: Why Supermarkets, Why Now?

### 2.1 Chronic Disease and Food Environments

Diet‑related chronic diseases (obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular conditions) are strongly shaped by food environments rather than individual willpower alone.[2][1]
Recent UK policy developments—including HFSS (high fat, salt, sugar) promotion and location restrictions and bans on advertising less healthy foods before 9 pm—explicitly recognise that supermarket shelf placement and marketing are **not neutral**, but structural drivers of dietary risk.[8][9][10]

### 2.2 Limits of Labelling and Education

Systematic reviews of front‑of‑pack nutrition labelling show that schemes like the UK traffic light label and Nutri‑Score improve objective understanding and can nudge choices towards healthier products, but their effects are uneven across socio‑economic groups and do not fully bridge comprehension gaps.[11][3][5]
Ingredient lists, nutrition panels and apps such as Yuka or FoodSwitch provide information, yet many consumers still misinterpret health claims or struggle to see how information translates into lived health, satiety, affordability and trust.[12][13]

### 2.3 Opaque Prices and Supply Chains

While cost‑plus grocery models (e.g., Cash Saver’s “cost plus 10%”) and true pricing experiments (e.g., Albert Heijn’s True Price trial) demonstrate that it is possible to disclose cost structures and environmental externalities at point of sale, the majority of supermarkets still conceal margins, supplier conditions and external costs, making “expensive” or “cheap” hard to interpret beyond emotion.[14][15][6][16]

### 2.4 Consumers as Shoppers, Not Food Citizens

Participatory food governance initiatives—food policy councils, citizen‑science mapping of food environments, co‑creation experiments in healthy retail—show that citizens are willing to engage in food democracy.[7][17][18]
However, these mechanisms rarely reach the supermarket floor: consumers remain primarily shoppers, and governance is mediated through purchasing power (which is unequally distributed) rather than through public evidence and collective decision‑making.[19][1]

**The project responds to this gap by designing a supermarket that embeds food evidence, price and supply‑chain transparency, citizen feedback and governance mechanisms directly into the shopping environment.**

## 3. Research Question and Objectives

### 3.1 Main Research Question

> By 2050, how might supermarkets turn food evidence into public transparency, healthier choices and shared accountability?

### 3.2 Specific Objectives

1. **Information and Explanation**: Transform food information (labels, ingredient lists, app scores, open databases) from mere disclosure into accessible explanation that supports understanding and comparative judgment.[13][5]
2. **Price and Supply‑Chain Transparency**: Make price composition and supply‑chain roles visible and discussable, so that “expensive” and “cheap” can be evaluated in terms of fairness and accountability, not only affordability.[15][6][20][14]
3. **Choice Architecture and Everyday Bundles**: Redesign shelf layouts and everyday meal deals so that default structures support satiety, metabolic health and clarity, while acknowledging pleasure and avoiding shame.[21][10][22]
4. **Food Governance and Citizen Participation**: Develop in‑store interfaces—community rating walls, evidence boards, supplier trust panels—that allow consumers to act as food citizens contributing public evidence and influencing procurement, reformulation and transparency standards.[17][18][7]

***

## 4. Conceptual Framework: Food Evidence, Accountability, Metabolic Health and System Change

The project integrates four strands of theory and practice:

1. **Food Evidence**: Treating labels, price breakdowns, supply‑chain maps, nutritional studies and consumer experiences as systematic evidence about how food systems shape everyday life.[23][3][1]
2. **Accountability**: Extending accountability beyond regulatory compliance to include transparent explanation of how health risks, costs and profits are distributed across products and actors.[4][6][17]
3. **Metabolic Health**: Linking nutritional quality, processing level, satiety and meal structure with chronic disease trajectories, using tools like Nutri‑Score and NOVA as operational proxies.[22][13]
4. **System Change**: Recognising supermarkets as leverage points in food systems; design interventions at the retail level can interact with policy, supply‑chain incentives and citizen governance to reshape norms.[24][2]

## 5. The Transparent Supermarket: Seven Spatial Zones

The design proposition is articulated through seven zones, each corresponding to specific project objectives and supported by existing international cases.

### 5.1 Manifesto Entrance

**Purpose**: Diagnose the problem and reframe identity—from “shopping as individual willpower” to “shopping within a designed food environment.”  
**Key messages**:

- “The ingredient list is not an explanation. Disclosure is not clarity. Compliance is not care.”[5][12]
- “This is not a failure of willpower. This is a designed food environment.”[9][1]

**Design features**:

- Large bilingual manifesto poster articulating project principles.  
- Infographics showing HFSS location restrictions and less‑healthy food advertising bans, highlighting that shelves and promotions are regulated, not neutral.[10][8][9]
- A short narrative explaining that the supermarket will be used as a site for public health and food governance.

### 5.2 Food Transparency Zone

**Purpose**: Turn food from a black box into a visible system—ingredients, processes, tests, origins and price composition.[20][25]

**Design features**:

- **Price breakdown boards** showing approximate shares of raw materials, processing, logistics, losses, certification, supermarket margin and supplier margin for selected products, plus external cost components derived from true pricing.[6][14][15]
- **Food dissection displays** that unpack the composition and processing steps of selected ultra‑processed foods, together with origin and safety testing information.[13][20]
- **True Price corner** offering dual prices (normal and “true”) for specific items, with explanation of the environmental and social costs embedded in the “true price,” inspired by Albert Heijn’s True Price pilot.[26][16][15]

### 5.3 Nutrition Translation Zone

**Purpose**: Convert labels from numeric disclosure into interpretive tools that integrate nutritional quality and processing level, while exposing the limits of health claims.[3][13]

**Design features**:

- Comparative panels explaining UK traffic light labels, Nutri‑Score and NOVA processing groups, and how they differ and complement each other.[27][28][5]
- Examples drawn from Open Food Facts showing combined Nutri‑Score and NOVA ratings for real products, plus screenshots of apps like Yuka that translate these into simplified scores.[23][13]
- A “health halo gallery” displaying packaging with claims such as “high protein,” “low fat,” “natural,” and explaining research on how these claims can mislead consumers.[3]

### 5.4 Sort by Need Zone

**Purpose**: Reorganise shelves based on human needs—satiety, price honesty, label clarity, processing level and trust—rather than brand dominance and promotion budgets.[10][22]

**Design features**:

- Product‑level scorecards showing satiety (based on protein, fibre and energy density), NOVA processing level, price honesty (presence of cost breakdown or true price), and trust score (supply‑chain transparency and community ratings).[28][22][17]
- Shelf layouts that place higher‑satiety, lower‑processing and more transparent products at more prominent positions, while still acknowledging pleasure items without shaming.[29][30]
- A “sorting control panel” indicating current weightings (e.g., Satiety 40%, Price Honesty 30%, Trust 30%) and allowing community input via the Food Governance Zone.[7][19]

### 5.5 Transparent Meal Deal Zone

**Purpose**: Redesign everyday UK‐style meal deals so they become transparent, more satiating and less shame‑inducing, turning “cheap convenient calories” into accountable daily bundles.[21][22]

**Design features**:

- A structural shift from main + snack + drink towards **1–2 filling foods + 1 pleasure food + 1 clear drink**, with explicit information on energy, sugar, salt, fat and satiety.[31][22]
- Bundle cards summarising Nutri‑Score, NOVA, satiety indicators and price/external cost information for each meal deal.[15][13]
- Non‑shaming copy that acknowledges pleasure while resisting diet culture anxiety, e.g. “Pleasure is allowed. Confusion is not.”[32][1]

Relevant evidence includes critical analyses of UK meal deals showing that many combinations exceed recommended lunch energy and sugar targets and can be as calorific as fast‑food meals, as well as US “Make It Fresh, for Less!” bundling experiments demonstrating that bundling alone is insufficient without explanation and price logic.[33][31][22][21]

### 5.6 Food Governance Zone

**Purpose**: Enable consumers to transition from shoppers and complainers to **food citizens** who generate public evidence and influence retail decisions.[17][7]

**Design features**:

- **Community rating walls** where products and suppliers receive public scores and comments, aggregated from in‑store and app‑based input.[7][17]
- **Food evidence boards** collecting unclear labels, unexplained prices, misleading health claims and “hard‑to‑stop” products as public evidence rather than private guilt.[1]
- Information interfaces explaining how to connect with local food policy councils, municipal participatory processes and citizen‑science projects mapping food environments.[19][17]

### 5.7 Supply Chain Trust Board

**Purpose**: Extend accountability beyond the supermarket, making suppliers, processors and logistics actors visible and evaluated.[6][15]

**Design features**:

- Supplier scorecards showing stability, transparency, pricing fairness and environmental/social practices, referencing Fairtrade, B Corp, True Price and regenerative agriculture certifications.[34][6][17]
- Range charts displaying approximate distributions of farmer income, supplier profit and retailer margin for selected products, based on studies of cost and margin formation in food chains.[35][6]
- Traceability maps and verified claim badges inspired by Provenance and other blockchain‑based traceability pilots.[25][36][20]

## 6. Theory of Change and Stakeholders

### 6.1 Theory of Change Summary

The project’s Theory of Change can be summarised as:

- **Inputs**: Policy window (HFSS restrictions, advertising bans, soft drink levy), existing labels and databases (traffic light, Nutri‑Score, NOVA, Open Food Facts), transparency and traceability experiments (True Price, Cash Saver, Provenance), and participatory governance initiatives (food policy councils, citizen‑science).[37][9][20][15][23][7]
- **Activities**: Designing and prototyping the seven zones within supermarkets, co‑creating interfaces with retailers, policymakers, communities and researchers.[18][24]
- **Outputs**: Visible explanation interfaces, transparent price breakdowns, need‑based sorting shelves, redesigned meal deals, governance walls and trust boards.[14][22][13][17]
- **Outcomes**: Improved consumer understanding and trust, healthier purchasing patterns, more transparent and accountable suppliers, and supermarkets adopting health and transparency as part of their business logic.[38][39][40][1]
- **Impacts**: Reduced diet‑related chronic disease burden, transformation of retail systems from “health for profit” to “health and transparency compatible with profit,” and embedding consumers as food citizens in everyday governance.[2][1]

### 6.2 Stakeholders and Incentives

Key stakeholders include:

- **Government and Regulators**: Seeking structural tools to address chronic disease and health inequalities; can integrate transparent supermarkets into food strategy and regulation.[8][9][2]
- **Supermarkets and Retailers**: Facing consumer demand for deeper transparency and opportunities to differentiate via trust and health‑enabling environments.[41][40][38]
- **Food Brands and Suppliers**: Responding to regulatory and reputational pressures; can use transparency and fair pricing as brand assets.[6][17]
- **Consumers / Food Citizens**: Experiencing health anxiety and confusion; seeking clarity, fairness and participation.[39][32][1]
- **Researchers and Designers**: Developing co‑created healthy retail interventions and evaluating systems change.[42][18][24]
- **Media and Civil Society Organisations**: Advocating for transparency, fairness and health, and translating complex evidence for broader publics.[43][41]

The project explicitly explores how these actors can find “co‑benefits”—health improvements, trust, reputation, regulatory clarity and commercial viability—through the Transparent Supermarket blueprint.

## 7. Methodological Approach

### 7.1 Research and Design Methods

The project will combine:

- **Literature and Policy Review**: Synthesising evidence on labelling effectiveness, healthy choice architecture, price transparency, traceability and participatory governance.[30][3][6][7]
- **Case Study Analysis**: Detailed analysis of selected cases (UK HFSS restrictions, soft drinks levy, traffic light label, Nutri‑Score, Open Food Facts, Yuka, Cash Saver, Albert Heijn True Price, Provenance, Fairtrade, food policy councils, meal bundling interventions, healthy checkout trials).[44][37][9][18][33][5][15][23]
- **Speculative and Critical Design**: Developing spatial and interface prototypes for the seven zones, informed by evidence but intentionally future‑oriented (2035/2050 horizons).
- **Stakeholder and User Engagement**: Through interviews, workshops and possibly small‑scale prototyping with retailers and communities, to test acceptability, risks and opportunities.[18][24][42]

### 7.2 Expected Outputs for the Supervisor and Academic Work

- A conceptual framework situating supermarkets in food governance and chronic disease prevention.  
- A mapped blueprint of the Transparent Supermarket zones with evidence‑based rationales and case references.  
- A Theory of Change and stakeholder analysis clarifying pathways from interventions to outcomes.  
- Academic papers or chapters on: food transparency and governance; healthy retail design; and speculative infrastructures for food citizenship.

***

## 8. Contribution and Significance

This project contributes to debates in public health, food policy, design research and governance by:

- Moving beyond individualised “healthy eating advice” towards structural redesign of food environments.  
- Demonstrating how existing tools (labels, taxes, traceability platforms, governance councils) can be integrated into a coherent retail interface rather than treated as isolated interventions.[37][20][3][7]
- Proposing supermarkets as everyday civic interfaces where food evidence becomes public accountability and consumers act as food citizens.
