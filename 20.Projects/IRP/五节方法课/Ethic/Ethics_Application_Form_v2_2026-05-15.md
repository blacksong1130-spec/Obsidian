## Part 2 — Project Description

*Please provide a brief description of your project and the activities that it involves.*

This project investigates how everyday food environments and post-meal bodily signals shape dietary behaviour — and how design interventions might make "eating well" more perceptible, low-friction, and emotionally sustainable without relying on calorie counting or numerical scoring. The research examines two design concepts: Concept A (food environment structuring through defaults and spatial arrangement) and Concept B (a non-judgmental post-meal body-awareness prompt timed to the 20-minute satiety window identified by Robinson et al., 2014).

The research uses a four-phase qualitative design with adult participants aged 22–45 who have had any experience of trying to change eating habits or of using dietary tools. All phases take place in the UK. Data will be analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006/2022).

**Phase 1 — Spatial Observation, Shared Meal & Semi-Structured Conversation**
Approximately 90 minutes per participant · 3 participants · conducted individually
The researcher visits each participant's everyday eating environment (e.g., kitchen, dining area, or their usual takeaway eating space), makes observational notes on the space, shares a meal with the participant, and then conducts a recorded conversational interview covering: food environment and daily eating patterns; past experiences of trying to change habits; body sensations after meals; and previous use of dietary tracking tools.

**Phase 2 — One-Day Photo Diary**
Same day · same 3 participants from Phase 1
Within 15–25 minutes after each main meal on one agreed day, each participant: (1) photographs their post-meal environment (not the food itself); and (2) writes 3 brief descriptive sentences about their body sensations. Materials are shared via a private photo album or direct message to the researcher.

**Phase 3 — Mindful Group Lunch Session**
Approximately 60 minutes · 4–5 participants · one-off group session
Participants bring their own lunch to a shared table at RCA. The session includes: a brief written pre-meal questionnaire (hunger level, expectation, mood word); an observed shared meal; and a facilitated group discussion within the 20-minute post-meal satiety window. The group context is fully disclosed to participants in advance. An optional showing of Concept B sketches may follow.

**Phase 4 — Design Probe Interviews**
Approximately 40 minutes per participant · Phase 1/2 participants · conducted individually
Participants review printed photographs from their own photo diary, then respond to two speculative design concepts presented as hand sketches. No numerical ratings or formal assessments are used.

**Researcher Autoethnography (parallel, throughout all phases)**
The researcher maintains a personal reflective diary about her own eating experiences and research assumptions throughout the project. This involves the researcher only and does not require participant consent. It will be clearly labelled as researcher data and analysed separately from participant data in the final report.

## Part 4 — Management of Ethical Risks

*For any question where 'Yes' was answered above, please explain how you will manage the ethical risks involved.*

**Primary consideration: Involvement of human participants**

The project involves adult participants discussing their everyday food experiences and body sensations. While this topic has the potential to intersect with personal sensitivities around body image or eating habits for some individuals, the following safeguards have been implemented to minimise risk and protect participant wellbeing:

**1. Recruitment language and exclusion criteria**
Recruitment messaging is broad and non-stigmatising, inviting anyone who has tried to change eating habits or has used any dietary tool (app, notebook, meal plan). The messaging explicitly does *not* target people described as "struggling" with food, as this language could attract vulnerable participants and create undue ethical risk (per Shannon Larkin's guidance, 15 May 2026). The only explicit exclusion criterion is a *current clinical diagnosis of an eating disorder*.

**2. Interview and discussion design**
All conversation prompts across Phases 1, 3, and 4 use descriptive, body-neutral language. No weight, calorie, or moral framing ("good/bad food") is used at any point. Participants are guided to describe sensations (e.g., "light," "heavy," "settled," "restless") rather than to evaluate or judge their dietary choices.

**3. Group session confidentiality (Phase 3)**
The Phase 3 group session involves 4–5 participants who may be able to recognise each other. The participant information and consent form explicitly discloses the group setting in advance. All group participants are asked to treat other participants' contributions as confidential. The consent form includes a specific checkbox acknowledging this request.

**4. Participant autonomy and withdrawal**
All participants are fully briefed before any data collection begins. Participants may withdraw at any point before the project is submitted and are not required to give any reason. The consent form clearly states this.

**5. Recording and data management**
- **Recording consent**: Participants provide explicit consent for each format (audio recording / photography) via the signed consent form. Consent for one format does not imply consent for others.
- **Anonymisation**: No real names, identifiable images, or identifying details will appear in any report or presentation. Photo diary images will not be used publicly without the participant's express written permission.
- **Secure storage**: All audio recordings and photo materials are stored in a password-protected folder accessible only to the researcher. Audio recordings are transcribed and then deleted. All data is destroyed after the project is submitted.
- **Vulnerable groups**: Individuals with a current clinical eating disorder diagnosis are excluded from all phases.

**6. Researcher positionality**
The researcher's autoethnographic diary serves as a reflexivity tool and will be explicitly disclosed in the final report as researcher-generated data, separate from participant data (Ellis, Adams & Bochner, 2011).
